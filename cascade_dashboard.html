    1b · What That Heat Does to the Road Itself

    What to Say
    "Asphalt softens, cracks, and starts to buckle. We track how fast the damage gets worse depending on how hot it gets and how long the heat lasts."
    Asphalt Softening Point Standard asphalt binder softens at 46–52°C. Road surface at 60–65°C consistently exceeds this, causing rutting and surface deformation under traffic load.
    Structural Degradation Rate Each 5°C increase above softening point accelerates structural degradation by 15–20%. Nashville summers routinely push pavement past critical thresholds.
    Source: AASHTO pavement performance standards | Federal Highway Administration Heat Effects Guide

    2. Water Systems — The First Cascade

    Script Intro
    "Now here's where it starts to get interesting. The road problems don't stay on the road."

    2a · Heat Traveling Underground to Water Pipes

    What to Say
    "Water pipes run right under roads — usually just about a meter deep. When the road above gets extremely hot, that heat travels down into the ground and warms the pipes — rising 8 to 12°C at pipe depth."
    8–12°C
    Subsurface Temperature Increase at Water Main Depth
    Herb et al. (2008) — measured at 0.9–1.5m depth
    2× per 5°C
    Rate of Bacterial Growth Increase in Warmer Water
    WHO quality threshold: 25°C
    Physical Setup Pavement surface 60–65°C. Soil conducts heat downward. Water mains at 0.9–1.5m experience 30–35°C soil temperature — vs. normal 15–20°C under vegetated areas.
    Water Quality Risk Warmer water grows bacteria faster. Above 25°C: quality degradation begins. Above 30°C: 4× baseline bacterial rate. Critical for public health in a prolonged heat event.
    Source: Herb et al. (2008) Journal of Hydrology | Agudelo-Vera et al. (2020) Water

    2b · Pipe Stress and Where Nashville is Most at Risk

    What to Say
    "Older cast-iron pipes — and Nashville has many of these — are especially vulnerable. The combination of heat and uneven ground from road buckling can increase pipe failure rates by 30 to 50 percent."
    Nashville Vulnerability: ~45% of Nashville bridges were built pre-1970. Water mains from the same era are cast iron — the most vulnerable to thermal stress, corrosion, and differential settlement. The areas with old roads and old pipes overlap significantly.
    How Road Stress Reaches Pipes Pavement buckling creates uneven loading. Soil shifts transmit that movement to pipes. Surface load variability increases pipe stress by 15–40%. Differential settlement alone raises failure rate 30–50%.
    Failure Modes Joint separation from thermal cycling. Brittle fracture in aged cast iron. Corrosion pitting at elevated temperatures. Pressure surge damage during peak demand.
    Sources: Rajani & Tesfamariam (2007) | Wols & van Thienen (2014) | Kleiner & Rajani (2001)

    5b · Why Costs Multiply, Not Just Add

    What to Say — Most Important Slide
    "These costs don't just stack — they multiply. When systems fail together, the total is 20 to 50 percent higher than if you added up each system separately. That's because one failure slows down the response to another."
    Key Insight: Ignoring cascade effects underestimates true infrastructure costs by 50–100%. Traditional single-system analysis misses the majority of economic impact.
  • Direct transport damage (event example): $50,000
  • Water system impacts: +$15,000 (+30%)
  • Building HVAC failures: +$20,000 (+40%)
  • Electrical grid stress: +$10,000 (+20%)
  • Public health (EMS surge, hospitalization, workforce loss): +$18,000 (+36%)
  • Cascade amplification (feedback effects): +$15,000
  • Total compound cost: $128,000 vs. $50,000 single-system estimate — 2.56× real cost
    • Source: Buldyrev et al. (2010) Nature | Ouyang (2014) Reliability Engineering

    5c · How the Gap Widens Under Extreme Heat

    What to Say
    "This panel compares a moderate heat event versus an extreme one. The cost curves diverge fast — which is the core argument for early investment in resilience."
    The Case for Early Investment: The gap between moderate and extreme event costs grows non-linearly. Small reductions in peak heat (via cool pavement, shade, etc.) produce outsized economic benefits across all downstream systems.
    Sources: Schramm et al. (2020) MMWR | Ouyang (2014) | Nashville vulnerability multipliers

    Cascade Impact Timeline

  • Transportation: Hours to days — immediate impact, fastest recovery
  • Water systems: Days to weeks — delayed impact, moderate recovery
  • Building HVAC: Weeks to months — gradual degradation, slow recovery
  • Electrical grid: Hours (immediate) + months (equipment degradation)
  • Public Health: Day 1 peak (acute illness) → sustained weeks (workforce loss, chronic exposure)
    • Optimal Recovery: Prioritize transportation restoration first — reduces recovery time for all other systems by 30–40% (Zhu et al., 2017).
    Sources: Zhu et al. (2017) Structure and Infrastructure Engineering | Faturechi & Miller-Hooks (2015)

    6c · Vulnerable Populations & Nashville's Exposure Map

    What to Say
    "Not everyone faces equal risk. The elderly, outdoor workers, and residents of low-income neighborhoods with old housing and poor AC coverage bear the greatest burden — and these populations often overlap with the highest infrastructure degradation zones in Nashville."
    Elderly (65+) — 3× Mortality Risk Reduced thermoregulation capacity, higher rates of cardiovascular comorbidities, more likely to live alone. Nashville has ~130,000 residents 65+ — concentrated in areas with older housing stock and lower AC penetration.
    Low-Income & Outdoor Workers Less access to AC, higher outdoor exposure. Construction, landscaping, and logistics workers are most exposed. These groups also have lower access to healthcare, amplifying downstream mortality risk.
    Intervention Leverage Points: Pre-positioned cooling centers along degraded road corridors; EMS staging in high-vulnerability zones during heat advisories; hospital pre-surge protocols activated when pavement temps exceed 60°C. These measures can reduce cascade mortality by 30–40% at low marginal cost.
    Sources: Schramm et al. (2020) MMWR | NIOSH Heat Stress Guidelines | Harlan et al. (2006) Social Science & Medicine
